# Think through a recommendation before writing the lines

Use this guide for sparse briefs and for improving detailed supplied drafts. Apply
[intent and script development](intent-and-script-development.md): preserve required information and explicit
locks, not automatically every word or timestamp. The aim is useful, emotionally engaging advice from a
particular person to a particular listener.
Conversational wording cannot rescue a script that has no reason to recommend the product.

## Keep the persuasive intent; make the content human

Claim, problem or question, proof, offer and CTA can remain the underlying persuasive chain. Human and
personal describe how those intentions are expressed: through a particular person's perspective, a
recognizable situation, useful observation and an activity the viewer wants to follow. The writing task
is to turn the required information into engaging content, not to remove commercial logic or merely
soften slogans with casual words. Plan the functions privately, then write a coherent conversation;
do not expose the planning labels as five disconnected pitches. Preserve required functions and exact
copy, while allowing their arrangement and expression to fit the brief.

Use the [latte reference script](recommendation-examples.md) to see this at work: curiosity motivates an
actual activity, the product belongs inside that activity, and a concrete reaction leads to an invitation.
The reference has no discount; an offer in a different brief still needs an appropriate place in its conversation.

## Find the reason this person wants to share

Start with the viewer's situation, not a list of things the brand wants said. What are they trying to do,
what small inconvenience or uncertainty gets in the way, and what would make that moment better? Choose
only a situation the product can credibly address. It may be getting ready, choosing a gift, finishing a
task, making something enjoyable, or deciding whether an unfamiliar product is worth understanding.
Do not invent a dramatic problem when curiosity, taste or practical convenience is the real motivation.

Give the speaker a point of view: why does this detail interest them, what would they compare or check,
and why would they bring it up with this listener now? A recommendation can emerge through a first look,
a demonstration, a recipe, a choice between uses, or an explanation. It need not start with a verdict.
Audience fit comes from recognizable priorities and vocabulary, not demographic stereotypes or borrowed slang.

Before drafting, be able to explain privately what makes the recommendation worth hearing without the
brand name, discount or generic praise. If the answer is only "it is good / convenient / a game changer,"
the product understanding or reasoning is still missing. Do not make that private planning a rigid script format.

## Turn a fact into a reason that matters

Work out the connection between a supported product detail, what someone can actually do or observe with
it, and the consequence in that person's life. Then ask who would care, in what situation, and why this
detail affects their choice. These are reasoning questions, not five compulsory sentences or a fixed order.

In the latte reference, naming the milk identifies a product; making the drink gives it a role; describing
what the drink tastes like answers the curiosity that made the activity worth following. The viewer should
not have to supply the missing connection between product information and personal relevance. Conversely,
do not explain what the action already makes obvious; use the words for the reason, judgment, limitation
or context the image cannot convey.

Specificity may be functional, sensory, aesthetic or emotional. A color chosen for a particular recipient,
a control that is easier to reach during a task, or a way to avoid rummaging can carry more conviction than
several superlatives. Select the useful details rather than reading the entire specification sheet.
Where a meaningful qualification is supported, include it as part of helping someone choose; do not invent
a flaw to simulate honesty, or force every recommendation to contain an objection.

## Ground the life detail without inventing a life

Separate product facts, observed demonstrations, supplied customer testimony, and authored situations.
Real supplied experiences can provide detailed history within their actual attribution and context.
A fictional creator can have an authored taste, intention or conditional preference, but cannot become
evidence of a purchase, a month of use, repeat buying, medical improvement or a comparative test.

When history is absent, write around a supported current operation, a relatable task, or a conditional
choice: explain why the visible arrangement would suit that task. Do not reduce the alternative to
"Here is the product." Useful specificity is still possible without a fabricated testimonial.
Packaging photos do not establish taste, skin feel, absorption, curl longevity or effectiveness.
Likewise, an AI-generated demonstration illustrates the script; it does not verify the underlying claim.

If the brief provides only a product name and discount, research the actual product through
[product understanding](product-understanding.md). Resolve a material missing fact before building a
recommendation around it. Do not fill the gap with invented personal results, but do not replace the
missing argument with a longer offer recital either. Keep necessary questions focused on what changes the script.

## Make the sentences develop a thought

Write as if the listener is following a decision, not waiting for the next slogan. Each sentence should
advance understanding: establish a need, notice a detail, explain its significance, answer a likely question,
qualify a choice, or help the listener act. Let the next sentence arise from the thought just introduced.
If the clauses could be shuffled without changing anything, they may still be a benefits list.

Use specific nouns and verbs, natural contractions, manageable clauses and occasional short reactions where
they have a cause. A quick aside, self-correction or expectation shift can express a real thought; adding
"honestly," "you guys," "obsessed," hesitation, or artificial mistakes everywhere is not human writing.
Do not copy a reference creator's verbal tics or assume a single dialect for every audience.

Information density means useful progress per moment, not merely words per second. A reason, a practical
instruction, a distinction between options and a relevant sensory description each do different work.
Cut repeated praise before cutting the explanation of why a detail matters. A short version should retain
one developed reason rather than compressing several reasons into unsupported claims. A longer version
needs additional useful understanding, not slower delivery of the same lines.

## Let feeling follow the discovery

The persuasive energy comes from wanting the listener to understand something worthwhile. Direct the
speaker's changing intention: recognizing a familiar annoyance, drawing attention to a useful detail,
concentrating on an operation, enjoying what it reveals, then offering a considered recommendation.
That progression creates opportunities for warmth, delight, relief, humor or conviction without constant hype.

Write vocal direction separately from exact dialogue. Tie emphasis to the phrase that carries the new
meaning; allow an operation or reaction time to register. Avoid identical excitement on every feature,
a sales-announcer finish, a smile held through every sentence, or whispered intimacy unrelated to the person.
Do not prescribe a stock emotional arc or pause pattern for every script. Read the whole speech naturally
and revise awkward syntax before trying to solve it with performance adjectives.

## Make the demonstration part of the explanation

For each important product moment, decide what the viewer needs to understand, which supported action
reveals it, what the speaker says while doing it, and what the viewer can conclude afterward. Merely holding
the logo toward the lens does not demonstrate usefulness.

Let the viewer see the relevant state before, during and after a meaningful operation. Direct attention to
the control, opening, texture, fit or result at the moment it matters, with physically feasible hand and
camera positions. A face-to-product glance and return to the listener can connect explanation and reaction.
Allocate real time for opening, applying, fitting or showing; do not fit a long instruction into an instant gesture.

One continuous phone shot may be enough. Use a motivated product insert when it clarifies something the
main framing cannot show, not to decorate every sentence. A result image may show appearance; it cannot
establish an unsupported efficacy claim or a genuine before/after trial. A demonstration must not imply
knowledge beyond the evidence ledger. See [sequence and prompts](sequence-and-prompts.md).

## Integrate commercial requirements into the conversation

Classify the brief's requirements: exact spoken wording, exact on-screen wording, or information that must
be conveyed. Do not silently move a spoken requirement into graphics or omit required copy. If the channel
is unspecified, make the proposed allocation clear in the script. Respect the user's explicit format.

A verified price, code or guarantee can help a viewer decide after understanding the relevance, or explain
why the speaker is bringing up a previously relevant choice now. An early offer is not automatically a
failure; it becomes a sales recital when there is no useful reasoning around it. Keep required phrases exact
inside natural sentences where possible, and make the offer's conditions clear without inventing urgency.
Do not turn every required phrase into a separate headline or vocal climax.

Choose a next step that follows the conversation and the actual brief: view the item, try a demonstrated
idea, compare an option, or use the supplied offer. Warm, helpful persuasion should not conceal sponsorship,
fabricate independence or pretend the generated person is an actual customer. Preserve required disclosures.
Natural recommendation and commercial transparency can coexist.

## Check the writing independently of the render

Read the speech without camera instructions, music or "enthusiastic" labels. Does it contain a specific
reason someone would want to share this, with enough detail for the listener to understand why? Can you
identify what each important sentence contributes? Would swapping the product leave almost everything intact?
Is the evidence supporting both the words and the implied visual conclusion?

Then read it with the planned handling. Do the hands demonstrate the point being made, and do speech and
action leave each other enough time? Is the offer integrated rather than substituting for the recommendation?
These are judgment prompts, not a scoring formula. Honor a locked script; flag a substantive concern instead
of silently rewriting it. See [latte reference script](recommendation-examples.md) for reasoning in context.
